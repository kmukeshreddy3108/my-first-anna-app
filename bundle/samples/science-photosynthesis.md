# Photosynthesis and the carbon cycle

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and some bacteria capture
light energy and store it as chemical energy. In green leaves, chlorophyll
absorbs mostly red and blue light. Water, H₂O, enters through the roots and
travels through vessels to the leaves. Carbon dioxide, CO₂, enters through
stomata. In simplified form, light energy helps combine CO₂ and H₂O to make
glucose and oxygen, O₂. The balanced equation is often written as
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, although the real pathway contains many steps.

The light-dependent reactions occur in thylakoid membranes. They split water,
release O₂, and produce energy carriers. The Calvin cycle occurs in the
stroma. It uses carbon dioxide and those energy carriers to build sugars.
Temperature affects enzyme activity: a plant may photosynthesize more quickly
as temperature rises, until heat damages proteins or increases water loss.
Many plants have an optimum near a moderate range, not an unlimited response.

The carbon cycle moves carbon among the atmosphere, living organisms, soil,
oceans, and rocks. Plants remove atmospheric CO₂ during photosynthesis.
Respiration returns CO₂ when organisms use stored food. Decomposition releases
carbon from dead material, while combustion transfers carbon from fuels to the
atmosphere. Oceans absorb and release CO₂, and some carbon becomes sediment.

Human activity changes the balance by burning coal, oil, and gas and by clearing
forests. More atmospheric CO₂ can increase photosynthesis for some plants, but
it also contributes to warming and ocean acidification. A useful approximation
is that growth depends on several limits together: light, water, nutrients,
temperature, and CO₂. A graph may show x²-shaped growth only in a mathematical
model, not a universal biological law. Scientists compare measurements and
uncertainty; “≈” does not mean a number is exact. Smart quotes, an em—dash,
°C, and µm can appear in notes without changing the underlying science.
