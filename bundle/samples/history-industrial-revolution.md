# The Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain during the late eighteenth
century and spread through Europe, North America, and parts of Asia during the
nineteenth century. It changed production from small workshops and households
to factories using machines and concentrated labor. The process was not one
event with one date. Textile mechanization accelerated after the 1760s, James
Watt improved the steam engine in the 1770s, and railways expanded rapidly
after the 1820s.

Several causes reinforced one another. Britain had coal and iron, commercial
capital, expanding markets, ports, and a relatively stable patent system.
Agricultural changes increased food production and displaced some rural
workers. Population growth supplied both workers and consumers. Inventors
adapted water power, steam power, spinning machines, and mechanical looms.
Canals and railways reduced the cost and time of moving fuel and goods.

Factories increased output and made products cheaper, especially cotton
textiles. Urban populations grew as people moved toward industrial jobs.
Manchester became a major textile center. Industrialization also created
engineers, managers, merchants, and a larger wage-earning working class.
These changes supported economic growth, but benefits were uneven.

Working conditions could be dangerous. Adults and children often worked long
hours in poorly ventilated mills and mines. Crowded cities faced pollution,
unsafe water, and disease. Reformers documented these conditions, and
governments gradually passed factory acts, public-health measures, and limits
on child labor. Workers formed trade unions and sometimes resisted machines,
as in the Luddite protests of 1811–1816.

The consequences were global. Industrial powers gained military and commercial
advantages, while imperial expansion supplied raw materials and markets.
Railways, telegraphs, and steamships connected distant economies. At the same
time, coal combustion increased air pollution and greenhouse-gas emissions.
The Revolution therefore produced both higher productivity and serious social,
political, and environmental problems.
